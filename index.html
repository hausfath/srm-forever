SRM Forever?

Could it make economic sense to decarbonize slowly while holding global temperature at 1.5 °C with stratospheric aerosol injection for as long as it takes, rather than mitigating rapidly and drawing temperatures back down with carbon removal? Both paths reach zero emissions and end at 1.5 °C — they differ in how fast, and in how long the commitment runs. In the SRM scenario, deployment continues until slow post-emission cooling (the ZEC drift) returns the underlying climate to the target on its own. A deliberately simple TCRE-based model; every number on this page recomputes from the inputs below.

Inputs

Defaults: SAI cost anchor from Smith (2020) with Niemeier & Timmreck (2015) forcing saturation (exponent 1.3), AR6 TCRE likely range, ZEC anchored at −0.1 °C per 1,000 years.

Abatement is priced as a vintage annuity: each increment of abatement pays the mitigation price for the financing period after it is built, then reaches cost parity. Both scenarios therefore pay the same total for the same complete decarbonization (40 GtCO₂/yr × price × period) — they differ only in when they pay it, so the discount rate carries the comparison. The accounting toggle shifts displayed levels only; the scenario difference and breakeven rate are identical under both.

How much does year 12,000 count? The discount-rate debate

Everything above ultimately hinges on one number. The mitigation scenario spends its money over decades to a few centuries; the SRM scenario spreads a smaller annual bill over sixteen millennia. Comparing them requires deciding how much a cost borne in the year 12,000 counts against a dollar spent today — and that decision, not the climate physics, is what flips the verdict.

One camp, associated with Nordhaus, says the answer should be read off markets. Capital is productive: a dollar invested at historical returns of 4–5% grows into far more than a dollar, so the cheapest way to help the future is to invest now and let compounding do the work. This “descriptive” view has real force over decades. Over millennia it frays: the compensating investment is hypothetical, since no fund, trust, or state has ever carried a transfer across even a tenth of these horizons, and market rates reveal the preferences of people trading over years, not the interests of people ten thousand years out who are not present to bid.

The other camp, running from Ramsey (1928) to the Stern Review, holds that discounting the welfare of future people simply because they are future is, in Ramsey’s words, “ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination.” Stern set pure time preference at 0.1%/yr — justified only as the annual risk that no one is around to bear the cost — and discounted at about 1.4% overall. The standard rejoinder is that literal zero discounting has its own pathologies over unbounded horizons: any present sacrifice, however crushing, can be justified by an arbitrarily small benefit that lasts forever — a tyranny of the future to match the tyranny of the present.

Two further arguments matter specifically at this model’s timescale. First, the standard formula (discount rate = time preference + inequality aversion × growth) leans on future people being richer than us; sustained growth for ten thousand years implies consumption multiplying by factors with dozens of zeros, so either growth saturates and the rate falls toward pure time preference, or extra consumption stops mattering — both undermine constant market-like rates. Second, a constant rate makes an extreme quantitative claim without meaning to: at 1%/yr, a cost in the year 12,000 counts as ~10−44 of the same cost today. Read as a survival probability — the only coherent long-run reading of pure time preference — that asserts near-certainty that the obligation will never come due. Few people would defend that claim outright; a constant discount rate asserts it silently.

This tool adopts Weitzman’s resolution, which does not require settling the debate. If the “right” rate is uncertain — across economic theories, ethical positions, and possible futures — then the correct object to average is the discount factor, not the rate. Low-rate possibilities dominate far-future present values, so the certainty-equivalent rate starts at the mean μ and declines toward zero as the horizon grows (Weitzman 1998; 2001). The disagreement itself becomes the σ input: Drupp et al. (2018) surveyed over 200 experts and found a median long-run social discount rate of 2% with wide, persistent spread. Declining schedules are also institutional practice, not just theory: Arrow and twelve coauthors (2013) recommended them for US policy, and the UK Treasury’s Green Book mandates a stepped decline (3.5% to 2.5% across the first 125 years in the 2026 edition, with deeper declines toward ~0.9% in its long-horizon sensitivity guidance). We chose the Weitzman form over an official schedule because it is transparent, two-parameter, and honest about its own basis: μ is your central rate, σ is how much you concede the debate is unresolved, and σ = 0 recovers the conventional constant rate.

The deeper point is that no measurement settles this. Market interest rates are facts, but whether they are the right weights for people who cannot participate in those markets is an ethical judgment — a claim about the moral standing of the unborn, dressed in the notation of finance. That is why this page treats the discount rate as an input rather than a conclusion, and why the breakeven tile is arguably the most important output: under plausible uncertainty about the rate, the SRM-forever question lands squarely inside the range over which serious people actually disagree. The model can show what your ethics imply; it cannot choose them for you.
